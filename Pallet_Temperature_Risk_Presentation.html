Goal

What question are we answering?

If a pallet of chicken boxes is stacked in layers, which positions become risky first, which layers are most vulnerable, and how much uncertainty is there around that risk?

  • Observed state-space data tells what happened in the measured trials.
  • Monte Carlo simulation data tells how much the result can vary under uncertainty.
  • Risk threshold used so far is value >= 4.
  • Practical output is a ranked list of risky layers, risky boxes, and when risk begins.

Observed result

Layer mean trend from observed state-space data

Data used: layer_hour_summary.csv, generated by averaging all observed trial values for each layer at each hour.
What it shows: mean observed value over time for Layers 1-4. The dashed line marks the risk threshold of 4.
How to read it: the higher the line, the greater the measured temperature/loss value. A layer crossing the dashed line has entered the risk zone on average.
Main result: Layer 1 rises fastest and is highest by hour 24. Layer 4 becomes the second-highest layer. Layer 3 remains safest overall.

Observed risk over time by layer

Data used: layer_hour_risk_summary.csv, where every observation is marked risky if value >= 4.

Main result: Layer 1 enters risk earliest. Layer 4 starts lower but becomes strongly risky by the end. Layers 2 and 3 remain lower, with Layer 3 lowest.

Monte Carlo risk trend by layer

Data used: monte_carlo_layer_hour_summary.csv, specifically the percentage of simulations where simulated_value >= 4.
What it shows: risk of loss percentage over time for each layer.
How to read it: 0% means no simulations crossed the threshold. 100% means every simulation crossed it.

Monte Carlo result

Monte Carlo hour-24 box risk ranking

Data used: monte_carlo_hour24_box_risk_ranking.csv, using each box's Monte Carlo risk percentage at hour 24.
What it shows: a ranked probability-of-loss chart for every box at hour 24. Bar colors identify layers.
How to read it: bars near 100% are almost always above threshold in simulation and should be treated as high-risk positions.
Main result: L1B2 and L1B5 reach 100% simulated risk. L1B1 is 99.8%. Important non-Layer-1 hotspots include L4B18, L4B20, and L2B6.
Monte Carlo result

Hour-24 uncertainty by box

Data used: monte_carlo_hour24_box_risk_ranking.csv, using each box's mean simulated value plus the 5th and 95th percentiles.
What it shows: each dot is the hour-24 simulated mean. The horizontal interval shows likely uncertainty from the 5th to 95th percentile. The dashed line is threshold 4.
How to read it: if the dot is right of the threshold, the average case is risky. If most of the interval is right of the threshold, the risk is robust.
Main result: Layer 1 boxes sit clearly beyond the threshold. Several Layer 4 boxes also extend far into risky values, showing strong uncertainty but real concern.

Combined interpretation

What we can confidently say right now

  1. Layer 1 is the primary risk layer. It has the highest observed hour-24 mean and the highest Monte Carlo hour-24 risk.
  2. Layer 4 is the second major concern. It starts lower but becomes high-risk by hour 24, especially at boxes L4B18 and L4B20.
  3. Layer 3 is the safest layer overall. It has the lowest observed final mean and lowest Monte Carlo risk percentage.
  4. There are individual box hotspots outside Layer 1. L2B6 and L4B18 deserve attention even though their layers are not always highest overall.
  5. Risk is spatial, not only time-based. Coordinates, layer, and exposed surfaces all appear relevant to where heat/loss accumulates.
Operationally, the pallet should not be treated as one uniform block. Layer and box position matter.
Open item

What is not completed yet

The next planned analysis is a direct comparison between the observed state-space results and the Monte Carlo simulation results.

  • Compare observed mean vs Monte Carlo mean for every box-hour.
  • Compare observed hour-24 ranking vs simulated risk ranking.
  • Identify where Monte Carlo overestimates or underestimates observed values.
  • Flag boxes that are consistently risky in both observed and simulated views.
The needed files already exist: state_space_combined_clean.csv and monte_carlo_combined_clean.csv. This deck covers work completed up to, but not including, that comparison step.
Recommendation

Recommended next work

  1. Run state-space vs Monte Carlo comparison. This will show whether simulations are aligned with actual observed trial behavior.
  2. Build a combined pallet risk dashboard. Add observed trends, Monte Carlo risk, heat maps, and box rankings into one interactive view.
  3. Test multiple risk thresholds. The current threshold is 4; testing 3, 5, and 6 will show whether conclusions are stable.
  4. Model position effects. Use layer, X/Y/Z coordinates, hour, exposed surfaces, and trial to predict risk.
  5. Translate results into holding-time guidance. For example: which boxes/layers become risky by hour 12, 18, and 24.
The most valuable next deliverable is the observed-vs-simulated comparison, because it tells us how much trust to place in the Monte Carlo risk estimates.
