Setup

What we're measuring, and why it matters

Thermocouple

A direct core/body-temperature probe. This is the number we actually care about for the bird — our "ground truth."

The value every other sensor is being judged against.

IdentiCool

An internal reference probe, placed differently from the thermocouple. Already fairly close to the animal.

Candidate #1 as a stand-in for the thermocouple.

Wireless sensor

Ambient house/air temperature. Fully non-invasive and easy to deploy at scale, but physically once-removed from the bird.

Candidate #2 as a stand-in for the thermocouple.
If ambient air alone could reliably predict core body temperature, we could monitor bird welfare non-invasively and at scale, without a probe on or near the animal. That practical question is behind every chart in this deck.
The question

What we're actually answering

"If we only had the IdentiCool reading, or only had the ambient wireless reading, how accurately could we estimate what the thermocouple would show — and is a time delay part of the story?"

  • Tested at two conditions — 30°C and 37°C — since the relationship might behave differently at each.
  • Tested with six different prediction methods per case, so the conclusion isn't an artifact of picking the wrong method.
  • Judged on the same four metrics every time, explained on the next slide.
How to read the results

The four numbers you'll see on every chart

R² (R-squared)

Of all the up-and-down movement in the real thermocouple reading, what percentage does our prediction actually explain? 0% = no better than guessing the average every time. 100% = perfect.

This is our main scoreboard number. Above ~0.5 is a genuinely useful relationship for noisy biological data; near 0 (or negative) means the model isn't working.

Pearson r (correlation)

How tightly the two sensors move together in a straight line, from −1 (perfectly opposite) through 0 (no relationship) to +1 (perfectly together).

Easier to picture than R², but on its own it doesn't say how big the error is in real degrees — we always look at it alongside RMSE.

RMSE (root mean squared error)

The typical size of the mistake, in real °C. If RMSE = 2°C, the prediction is usually off by about 2 degrees. Lower is better.

Punishes occasional big misses more heavily than small, consistent ones.

MAE (mean absolute error)

Also the average mistake in °C, but every error counts equally — a few large misses don't dominate the number.

RMSE and MAE close together = errors are fairly consistent; RMSE much higher than MAE = a few bad misses are dragging it up.
Ranking rule used throughout: highest R² first, then highest Pearson r, then lowest RMSE. One metric alone can be misleading, so we never pick a "winner" on a single number.
Method

Why test six different prediction methods, not one?

No single method is right for every relationship — some data behaves in a straight line, some bends. Testing all six and letting the numbers decide avoids baking in a bad assumption.

Linear Regression

The simplest possible straight-line relationship between the two sensors.

Ridge Regression

Also a straight line, but adjusted so a few noisy/odd readings can't throw it off as easily.

Random Forest

Many simple decision rules voting together — good at catching bends and irregular patterns.

Gradient Boosting

Decision rules that learn from each other's mistakes step by step — often the most accurate, but can latch onto noise if the dataset is small.

KNN (k-Nearest Neighbors)

Predicts a new reading by looking at what happened during the most similar past readings.

SVR (Support Vector Regression)

Fits a flexible trend line that tolerates small errors while still respecting the overall pattern.

On every results slide that follows, all six are shown side by side — the "winning" model is simply whichever one scored best on that specific data, not a method we favored going in.

Step 5 · The twist

Why we suspected a time delay

Heat doesn't move instantly. Air around a bird needs time to actually change its core body temperature. If the same-instant ambient reading barely predicts core temperature, that could mean air temperature is irrelevant — or it could mean we're comparing the wrong moments in time.

New question: does ambient temperature from 10, 20, or 30 minutes ago predict core temperature right now better than the ambient temperature at this exact instant?
  • Re-ran the wireless comparison using three lagged features per prediction: wireless reading at t−10, t−20, and t−30 minutes.
  • Same six models, same four metrics, same ranking rule — only the input timing changed.

Step 6 · Sanity check

Does a real law of physics explain the lag?

The lag models found a strong statistical pattern — but statistics alone don't prove a mechanism. We tested whether a textbook law of heat transfer, Newton's Law of Cooling, reproduces the same behavior on its own: core temperature drifts toward ambient temperature at a rate proportional to how far apart they currently are.

dTcore/dt = k × (Tambient − Tcore) — in words: "the bigger the gap between air and core temperature, the faster the core temperature moves to close it."
  • One-step check: does the formula predict the very next reading correctly?
  • Recursive check (the honest test): starting from one known point, let the formula simulate forward entirely on its own, the way it would have to work in real-time use.

Recommendation

What we should do with this

  1. For an immediate, same-instant estimate with no extra math, IdentiCool is the more reliable proxy today (R² 0.43–0.70 vs 0.04–0.48 for same-instant wireless).
  2. If a short delay is acceptable — or a 10–30 minute correction can be built into the workflow — the ambient wireless sensor becomes the single best predictor overall (R² 0.72–0.83), beating IdentiCool while being fully non-invasive.
  3. The physics-based model isn't ready to replace the data-driven lag model yet — it fits well at 37°C but breaks down at 30°C.
  4. 37°C is consistently easier to model than 30°C across every single pipeline tested — worth flagging for future data collection.
Bottom line: IdentiCool is the safer choice today for instant readings, but wireless + a lag correction is the more promising long-term path if non-invasive, remote sensing is the goal.
Open questions / next steps

Where this goes next

  • Formally tune the lag window (test delays beyond 10/20/30 min) to find the optimal delay per condition.
  • Investigate why the physics model diverges at 30°C — likely a fitting/data-volume issue, not a fundamentally wrong mechanism.
  • Collect more 30°C samples to check whether the weaker fit is a data-volume issue rather than a real physical difference.
  • Build out the dashboard (wireframe already drafted) so these comparisons can be re-run and viewed without opening scripts.
